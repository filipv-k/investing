Analysis of SAP's Litigation Challenges in Celonis v. SAP and Teradata v. SAP
I. Executive Summary
SAP SE (SAP), a global leader in enterprise application software, currently faces significant legal challenges in the United States federal courts through two concurrent, high-stakes lawsuits initiated by major competitors in adjacent software markets. The first, Celonis SE, et al. v. SAP SE, et al. (Case No. 3:25-cv-02519), filed in the Northern District of California (N.D. Cal.), involves allegations by process mining leader Celonis that SAP is engaging in anticompetitive conduct to monopolize the process mining market, primarily by leveraging its dominance in the Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) sector following its 2021 acquisition of Signavio.1 The second, Teradata Corp., et al. v. SAP SE, et al. (Case No. 3:18-cv-03670-WHO), also pending in N.D. Cal., centers on allegations by enterprise data warehousing (EDW) provider Teradata concerning illegal product tying and trade secret misappropriation related to SAP's HANA database platform.7
Celonis's complaint outlines a broad antitrust challenge, accusing SAP of monopolization, attempted monopolization, illegal tying, bundling, and predatory pricing, alongside claims of false advertising and tortious interference.2 Central to these allegations are SAP's actions post-Signavio acquisition, including restrictive data access policies (such as the "Clean Core" strategy and changes impacting API access), alleged self-preferencing of Signavio, and purported breaches of assurances made to regulators regarding ecosystem openness and third-party data access fees.1 Teradata's lawsuit, while narrower in scope following appeal, focuses on claims that SAP illegally tied its S/4HANA ERP application to its HANA database and misappropriated Teradata's 'batched merge' trade secret, allegedly obtained during a purported joint venture, to accelerate HANA's development and foreclose Teradata from the EDW market.33
A critical development is the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit's December 2024 decision in the Teradata case, which reversed the district court's prior grant of summary judgment in favor of SAP.40 This reversal revived both Teradata's antitrust tying and trade secret misappropriation claims, finding triable issues of fact and notably validating the admissibility and significance of Teradata's expert economic testimony regarding market definition and market power.40 This ruling significantly strengthens Teradata's position and highlights the crucial role economic evidence will play in both cases. Furthermore, Celonis's allegations concerning broken regulatory commitments 2, if substantiated, could seriously undermine SAP's defense narrative.
Considering the revived strength of Teradata's claims post-appeal, the breadth and potential potency of Celonis's allegations (particularly regarding regulatory assurances and data access policies), and the common themes suggesting a potential pattern of leveraging ERP dominance, SAP faces substantial legal and strategic hurdles. Achieving favorable outcomes—defined as dismissal, settlement on highly favorable terms, or outright trial victory—in both of these complex, high-stakes litigations concurrently presents a significant challenge for the company. The confluence of revived claims in one case and broad, potent allegations in the other, set against an evolving tech antitrust landscape, indicates a difficult path forward for SAP across both fronts.
II. Comparative Analysis of the Lawsuits
SAP is currently defending against two significant lawsuits brought by competitors in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California. While both cases involve allegations of anticompetitive conduct stemming from SAP's dominant position in the ERP market, they differ in their specific claims, the adjacent markets at issue, and their procedural maturity.
A. Celonis v. SAP (Case No. 3:25-cv-02519, N.D. Cal.)
Filed in March 2025, this lawsuit brought by process mining leader Celonis presents a multi-faceted challenge to SAP's practices, particularly those implemented following its 2021 acquisition of competitor Signavio.1
* Claims: The complaint details eleven distinct counts.2 The core antitrust allegations, brought under the Sherman Act, Clayton Act, and California's Cartwright Act, include monopolization of the "SAP ERP Data Access Market," attempted monopolization of the "SAP Process Mining Market," illegal tying of SAP ERP Data Access to SAP's Signavio process mining software, illegal bundling of ERP applications, data access, and Signavio, and predatory pricing of Signavio.2 Beyond antitrust, Celonis asserts claims for federal and state false advertising (under the Lanham Act and California Business and Professions Code § 17500), intentional interference with both contractual relations and prospective economic relations, and broadly unfair competition under California Business and Professions Code § 17200.2
* Core Allegations: Celonis contends that SAP is leveraging its dominance in the ERP market, where customers face high switching costs 2, to exclude competitors like Celonis from the adjacent process mining market.1 This alleged exclusion campaign intensified after SAP acquired Signavio in 2021.1 Key tactics alleged by Celonis include 2:
   * Self-Preferencing Signavio: Integrating Signavio directly with SAP's ERP, bundling it within the RISE with SAP offering, and providing free trials or promotional pricing.2
   * Restricting Data Access: Imposing or threatening prohibitive fees for third-party data extraction, potentially leveraging SAP's Digital Access Adoption Program (DAAP) or "Indirect Access" policies.2 Implementing technical limitations, such as changing API access policies (e.g., restricting ODP API use 73, recommending then allegedly undermining OData APIs), pushing customers towards the expensive SAP Datasphere for third-party integration, and enforcing a restrictive "Clean Core" policy that allegedly prohibits efficient extraction methods used by Celonis (like RFC ABAP).2
   * Breaking Regulatory Assurances: Allegedly reneging on specific promises made to antitrust regulators during the Signavio acquisition review concerning maintaining an open ecosystem and not charging third parties like Celonis for data access.2
   * Spreading FUD (Fear, Uncertainty, Doubt): Making false or misleading statements to customers about the compliance, risks, or technical viability of using Celonis's software with SAP systems, while potentially exaggerating Signavio's capabilities.2
   * Coercion: Conditioning migration to or support for SAP's S/4HANA ERP platform on customers abandoning Celonis in favor of Signavio, or threatening to withhold support if customers use extraction methods deemed non-compliant with the Clean Core policy.2
* Procedural Status: The case is in its early stages before Judge Vince Chhabria in the N.D. Cal..12 The complaint was filed on March 13, 2025.8 Celonis subsequently filed a motion for a preliminary injunction, seeking immediate court intervention against SAP's alleged practices.10 SAP has responded by filing a Motion to Dismiss Celonis's complaint, with a hearing scheduled for May 22, 2025.12 Notably, Judge Chhabria has already commented on the case, granting an SAP motion for more time to respond to Celonis's filings and observing that Celonis's requests to seal parts of its filings appear "dramatically overbroad," encouraging narrower redactions.12 An Initial Case Management Conference is scheduled for June 20, 2025.12 SAP has not filed counterclaims against Celonis according to available docket information.8
B. Teradata v. SAP (Case No. 3:18-cv-03670-WHO, N.D. Cal.)
This litigation, initiated by Teradata in 2018, has a more protracted history and focuses on SAP's conduct in the EDW market, particularly concerning its HANA database.7
* Claims: Following the Ninth Circuit's decision, the primary claims being pursued by Teradata are:
   * Antitrust Tying (Sherman Act § 1): Alleging SAP unlawfully conditions the sale of its S/4HANA ERP application (the tying product) on the purchase of its HANA database (the tied product).38
   * Trade Secret Misappropriation (Defend Trade Secrets Act - DTSA, California Uniform Trade Secrets Act - CUTSA): Alleging SAP stole and improperly used Teradata's proprietary 'batched merge' method to develop HANA.33
   * Note on Copyright Claim: Teradata's original complaint also included a claim for copyright infringement related to HANA's development.33 While the district court denied SAP's motion to dismiss this claim based on the statute of limitations 37, its current status post-remand and relative importance compared to the revived antitrust and trade secret claims is less clear from the provided materials. SAP also filed patent infringement counterclaims against Teradata 35, which influenced the appellate jurisdiction path but are stayed pending resolution of Teradata's claims.38
* Core Allegations: Teradata alleges SAP initiated a joint venture (the "Bridge Project") under false pretenses in 2008, primarily to gain access to Teradata's decades of EDW intellectual property, including trade secrets related to its massively parallel processing architecture.33 Teradata claims SAP misappropriated this IP, particularly the 'batched merge' method, to rapidly develop its competing HANA database.33 Upon HANA's release, SAP allegedly terminated the joint venture and began leveraging its dominant position in the ERP market, particularly the mandatory migration to S/4HANA, to coerce customers into adopting HANA, thereby foreclosing Teradata from competing for EDAW business among SAP's large customer base.33 Teradata also alleges SAP restricts its ability to access customer data residing in SAP systems.33
* Procedural Status: The case is currently remanded to the N.D. Cal. under Judge William Orrick after a significant appellate phase.34
   * The district court had initially granted summary judgment to SAP on the tying and technical trade secret claims in November 2021.38
   * Teradata's appeal was initially filed with the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit due to SAP's pending patent counterclaims. However, the Federal Circuit determined it lacked jurisdiction because the patent counterclaims were not compulsory (i.e., not arising from the same transaction or occurrence as Teradata's claims) and transferred the appeal to the Ninth Circuit in August 2023.38
   * In a significant ruling on December 19, 2024, the Ninth Circuit reversed the district court's summary judgment on both the antitrust tying and trade secret misappropriation claims.40 The appellate court found the district court abused its discretion by excluding the testimony of Teradata's economic expert, Dr. John Asker, and held that with this testimony, Teradata raised triable issues of fact regarding SAP's market power and the anticompetitive effects of the alleged tie.40 It also found triable issues regarding whether Teradata properly designated its trade secret as confidential and whether SAP had a license to use it under their agreements.40
   * The case has been remanded to Judge Orrick for further proceedings, potentially including renewed discovery, further motions, settlement negotiations, or trial preparation.85 Available materials do not indicate a specific trial date has been set or that formal settlement talks are underway as of early 2025.85

	D. Initial Comparative Observations
The two lawsuits present distinct yet related challenges for SAP. Celonis's action is characterized by its breadth, launching a wide array of antitrust and related claims targeting SAP's conduct specifically around its Signavio acquisition and subsequent data access policies.2 This requires SAP to mount a defense on multiple legal fronts simultaneously. In contrast, the Teradata case, while originating from similar themes of ERP leverage and IP issues, is now more narrowly focused on the specific claims of illegal tying (S/4HANA-HANA) and trade secret misappropriation ('batched merge') that were revived by the Ninth Circuit.40
Procedurally, the cases are at opposite ends of the litigation lifecycle. Teradata carries significant momentum for the plaintiff following the Ninth Circuit's reversal.40 The appellate decision validated crucial expert testimony 40 and found triable issues on the core claims, significantly weakening SAP's prior summary judgment victory and potentially clearing the path toward trial or a less favorable settlement for SAP. Celonis, conversely, is in the nascent stages.8 While this presents SAP with opportunities for early dismissal via the pending motion 12, it also carries the immediate risk of an adverse ruling on Celonis's motion for a preliminary injunction 10, which could disrupt SAP's business practices related to Signavio and data access even before the case reaches full discovery or trial. The Ninth Circuit's reasoning in Teradata, particularly regarding expert testimony and market power in the context of SAP's ecosystem, will undoubtedly influence the arguments and judicial review in the early stages of the Celonis case.
III. SAP's Position in Celonis v. SAP: Strengths and Weaknesses
SAP's defense in the Celonis litigation involves navigating a complex web of antitrust, intellectual property-related (false advertising), and tort claims. The analysis below examines SAP's potential strengths and weaknesses concerning the core allegations.
A. Analysis of Antitrust Claims (Monopolization, Tying, Bundling, Predatory Pricing)
* Weaknesses for SAP:
   * Market Definition & Power: Celonis's core argument relies on SAP possessing significant market power, specifically in the alleged "SAP ERP Data Access Market," which it then leverages into the "SAP Process Mining Market".2 SAP's widely acknowledged strong position in the ERP market, coupled with high customer switching costs 2, lends plausibility to the leverage theory. Teradata, in its related suit, alleged SAP's market share in the top-tier ERP market could range from 60% to 90%.34 While SAP will contest these specific market definitions and power assessments, the recent Ninth Circuit decision in Teradata validated an expert economic approach to defining markets and assessing power within SAP's ecosystem 40, potentially making it more challenging for SAP to dismiss these foundational elements outright if Celonis employs similar expert methodologies.
   * Exclusionary Conduct Allegations: Celonis provides a detailed list of alleged exclusionary actions undertaken by SAP, particularly post-Signavio acquisition.2 These include specific changes to data extraction policies (allegedly banning efficient methods like RFC ABAP while pushing inferior or costly alternatives like OData or Datasphere), the implementation of restrictive policies like "Clean Core," conditioning ERP support or migration on the use of Signavio, bundling Signavio attractively or below cost, and directly coercing customers or threatening support withdrawal.2 This specificity strengthens the complaint beyond general accusations. The alleged conduct fits recognized patterns of potential anticompetitive leveraging by dominant platforms into adjacent markets, a subject of increased antitrust scrutiny globally.88
   * Tying and Bundling Claims: The specific allegations that SAP conditions essential ERP migration or support (tying product) on the use of Signavio (tied product) 2, and offers Signavio bundled with other products at effective below-cost prices 2, align directly with established antitrust theories of harm. The Teradata case precedent, where the Ninth Circuit revived a tying claim against SAP involving HANA 40, may provide persuasive authority, although the specific products and alleged mechanisms differ.
   * Predatory Pricing: The claim that SAP offers Signavio below cost 2 directly challenges the fairness of SAP's competitive strategy for its acquired process mining tool. Proving predatory pricing is notoriously difficult, requiring complex cost analysis, but the allegation adds another layer to the antitrust assault.
* Strengths for SAP:
   * Challenging Market Definition/Power: Despite Celonis's allegations and the Teradata precedent, SAP has strong grounds to contest the proposed market definitions. SAP will likely argue that the "SAP ERP Data Access Market" is not a distinct antitrust market and that the relevant "Process Mining Market" is much broader than just SAP-connected solutions, encompassing tools from various vendors across different platforms.105 By arguing for broader market definitions, SAP can aim to demonstrate that its market share is below the threshold required for monopoly power.45 Proving monopoly power remains a significant hurdle for any plaintiff.
   * Procompetitive Justifications: SAP can present legitimate business justifications for its actions. The integration of Signavio can be framed as creating a more seamless, end-to-end business transformation suite for customers, particularly within the RISE with SAP offering.109 Data access policies like "Clean Core" can be defended as necessary for ensuring system stability, security, and facilitating smoother upgrades for customers, rather than as tools for exclusion.75 Changes to API access can be positioned as standard technical evolution and platform management.73 Courts generally exhibit reluctance to interfere with product design and integration decisions, especially when plausible efficiency or security justifications exist.111
   * Burden of Proving Harm to Competition: A key defense for SAP will be to argue that Celonis must demonstrate harm to competition as a whole within the relevant market, not merely harm to Celonis as a competitor.45 SAP may introduce evidence of a vibrant process mining market with multiple players 105 or argue that Signavio competes effectively on its merits. SAP could also challenge Celonis's own market position or pricing strategies.11
   * Defending Bundling/Pricing Claims: Bundling can be procompetitive if it offers cost savings or efficiencies to customers. Predatory pricing claims require rigorous proof that prices are below an appropriate measure of cost and that SAP has a dangerous probability of recouping its losses after driving out competitors – a high legal standard. SAP will argue its pricing is competitive and reflects legitimate market strategies.
B. Impact of Signavio Integration and Data Access Policies (Clean Core, DAAP/Indirect Access, API changes)
* Weaknesses for SAP: These policies are the alleged instruments of exclusion in Celonis's narrative.1 The timing of their implementation or stricter enforcement after the Signavio acquisition 1 is a central pillar of Celonis's argument suggesting anticompetitive intent. Celonis alleges these policies are applied discriminatorily, favoring Signavio while imposing technical hurdles (e.g., pushing non-viable OData APIs, then deprecating them, then promoting costly Datasphere) 2 or prohibitive costs (via Indirect Access/DAAP interpretations) 2 on third parties. The concept of "Indirect Access" licensing itself has faced significant criticism and legal challenges as potentially anticompetitive.5 SAP's history of evolving these policies (introducing DAAP, extending it, banning ODP access for third parties) creates a narrative of shifting goalposts that Celonis can leverage.72
* Strengths for SAP: SAP can position these policies as integral to its overall strategy and necessary for managing its complex ecosystem. The "Clean Core" initiative can be defended as a best practice promoting long-term system health, easier upgrades, and cloud readiness for customers.75 Licensing models like DAAP can be presented as attempts to simplify and provide transparency around indirect usage, moving towards outcome-based pricing.116 API changes can be justified based on technical roadmaps, security updates, or performance considerations. SAP can maintain that while customers own their data 4, SAP retains the right to control access to its proprietary software systems and APIs, setting the terms for such access. They might argue that older methods relied upon by Celonis (like RFC ABAP) were never officially supported for this purpose or pose security/stability risks in modern architectures.2
C. Significance of Alleged Broken Assurances to Regulators
* Weaknesses for SAP: This allegation is particularly damaging from a narrative perspective.1 Celonis claims that SAP explicitly assured regulators during the Signavio acquisition review that the ecosystem would remain open and competitive, and specifically that third parties like Celonis would not face data access fees.2 The complaint even quotes an alleged SAP representation regarding "simple scanner access" being free.2 If Celonis can produce evidence supporting these claims (e.g., submissions to regulators, internal SAP communications), it significantly undermines SAP's credibility. It paints SAP's subsequent policy changes not as neutral business decisions, but as a deliberate reversal of commitments made to secure regulatory approval, strongly suggesting anticompetitive intent. Even if these assurances were informal or made to non-US regulators (like the German Bundeskartellamt 109), demonstrating such a reversal could sway judicial perception.
* Strengths for SAP: SAP's primary defense here will likely be factual and legal. They can argue that Celonis misinterprets or cannot prove these specific assurances were made, particularly to US regulators like the FTC or DOJ.135 Publicly available information from the German Bundeskartellamt's review indicates clearance without explicit conditions on these points, although it notes SAP confirmed simple scanner access was free under the Digital Access Model.128 SAP can argue that regulatory clearance implies the merger itself was not deemed anticompetitive.109 Furthermore, any assurances made, particularly informal ones, may not be legally binding commitments enforceable by a third party like Celonis in an antitrust suit. SAP can frame post-acquisition policy changes as necessary integration steps or responses to market dynamics, unrelated to prior statements made in a different context. Notably, significant shifts in US FTC/DOJ merger review policies occurred in mid-to-late 2021 138, after the Signavio acquisition closed in early 2021 134, making it less probable that stringent, forward-looking commitments regarding third-party access were formally imposed under the prior enforcement regime.
D. Assessment of False Advertising and Tortious Interference Claims
* Weaknesses for SAP: The success of these claims 2 hinges on the specific facts Celonis can uncover and present. If discovery yields internal SAP documents or credible testimony from customers demonstrating that SAP knowingly made false statements about Celonis's product compliance or Signavio's capabilities, or intentionally interfered to cause breaches of Celonis contracts, these claims could pose a significant threat.10 Such evidence would not only support these specific counts but also reinforce the narrative of anticompetitive intent underlying the antitrust claims.
* Strengths for SAP: SAP can characterize its statements about competitors as standard competitive rhetoric, opinions, or puffery, which are generally not actionable as false advertising. Proving the specific elements of tortious interference – including intent to interfere and causation of actual contract breach or economic harm – often presents a high evidentiary bar for plaintiffs. SAP will likely argue that any customer decisions to switch from Celonis to Signavio were based on independent business judgments considering factors like price, integration benefits, or feature sets, rather than any tortious interference by SAP.
E. Further Considerations (Celonis)
The allegation that SAP broke promises made to regulators 2 holds substantial strategic value for Celonis, potentially casting doubt on SAP's motives and the legitimacy of its subsequent actions, even if the promises lack formal legal enforceability in this private suit. This narrative allows Celonis to frame SAP's policy shifts regarding data access and Signavio integration not merely as business decisions, but as evidence of a calculated plan to eliminate competition, potentially implemented after misleading regulators. In antitrust litigation where intent can be a crucial factor, such a narrative could significantly influence judicial perception of SAP's conduct and make its proffered procompetitive justifications seem less credible.
Furthermore, SAP's "Clean Core" strategy 75, while promoted as a technical best practice, is portrayed by Celonis as a key tool of exclusion.2 Celonis alleges SAP defines "clean" in a manner that specifically disadvantages third-party tools relying on established integration methods (like RFC ABAP) while mandating the use of SAP-controlled alternatives (OData, Datasphere) that are allegedly less suitable or significantly more expensive for process mining.2 This transforms a seemingly neutral technical standard into alleged anticompetitive conduct. If Celonis can demonstrate that the specific application of the Clean Core policy lacks objective technical necessity and disproportionately harms competitors while favoring Signavio, it bolsters the claims of monopolization and illegal tying.
Finally, the choice of the Northern District of California as the venue 1, known for a high volume of antitrust filings 152, suggests a strategic decision by Celonis. However, Judge Chhabria's early admonition regarding Celonis's "dramatically overbroad" sealing requests 12 indicates a potentially high level of judicial scrutiny from the outset. This suggests that while the forum may be generally receptive to antitrust claims, success will depend on rigorous adherence to procedural rules and strong evidentiary support, tested early via SAP's pending motion to dismiss.12
IV. SAP's Position in Teradata v. SAP: Strengths and Weaknesses Post-Remand
The Ninth Circuit's reversal of summary judgment significantly reshapes the landscape of the Teradata litigation, reviving claims that the district court had previously dismissed and strengthening Teradata's overall position as the case returns to the district court.
A. Analysis of Antitrust Tying Claim
* Weaknesses for SAP:
   * Impact of Reversal: The vacating of the summary judgment win is a fundamental setback for SAP.40 The tying claim, previously dismissed, is now active and heading potentially towards trial.
   * Expert Testimony Admissibility: The Ninth Circuit's explicit finding that the district court abused its discretion by excluding Dr. Asker's testimony is critical.40 This testimony, deemed admissible by the appellate court, provides the necessary economic foundation for Teradata's claim. It establishes triable issues of fact regarding the definition of the tying market ("core ERP products for large enterprises") and the tied market ("EDW products with [online analytical processing] capabilities for large enterprises"), SAP's market power within the tying market (supported by evidence of high market share, high margins, and significant customer switching costs), and substantial anticompetitive effects in the tied market (evidentiary support for significant market foreclosure, potentially supracompetitive pricing for HANA, and regression analysis linking the tie to Teradata's losses).40
   * Legal Standard (Per Se vs. Rule of Reason): The Ninth Circuit's analysis suggests the tying claim could survive under either the modified per se rule or the rule of reason.40 The court specifically rejected SAP's attempt to categorize the tie as automatically subject only to the rule of reason and distinguished it from platform software integration cases like Epic Games and Microsoft, noting HANA is a standalone EDW product.40 This potentially lowers the burden for Teradata compared to a pure rule of reason analysis, as the modified per se rule focuses primarily on market power in the tying market and effect on commerce in the tied market.
* Strengths for SAP:
   * Challenging Expert Evidence: While Dr. Asker's testimony is now likely admissible, SAP retains the ability to challenge its substantive conclusions, underlying data, and methodological nuances before the jury.69 SAP will present its own competing expert economic analysis aiming to refute Asker's market definitions, assessment of market power, and conclusions regarding anticompetitive foreclosure and harm.
   * Procompetitive Justifications: SAP can strongly argue that requiring HANA for S/4HANA is not an anticompetitive tie but a legitimate product design choice driven by technological integration, performance optimization, and efficiency benefits for customers.45 Demonstrating tangible customer benefits resulting from the integration could persuade a jury that the arrangement is procompetitive.
   * Tied Market Arguments: SAP can continue to dispute the definition of the tied product and market, arguing that HANA serves multiple purposes beyond EDW or that the relevant EDW market is broader and more competitive than Teradata portrays.40 This could undermine Teradata's foreclosure calculations.
   * Lack of Coercion Defense: SAP can argue that customers choose the S/4HANA-HANA combination based on its merits and value proposition, not because they are coerced. The availability of different HANA license types (full-use vs. runtime) might be presented as evidence of customer choice, although Teradata would counter that the runtime license restricts data use with competitors.40
B. Analysis of Trade Secret Misappropriation Claim
* Weaknesses for SAP:
   * Reversal of Summary Judgment: As with the tying claim, the Ninth Circuit's reversal revives the trade secret claim.40
   * Confidentiality Designation: The appellate court found a genuine factual dispute regarding whether Teradata adequately designated the 'batched merge' method information as confidential.40 The court noted the relevant document was marked "Teradata Confidential" and rejected the notion that the non-disclosure agreement required highly detailed descriptions within the marked document itself.40 This significantly undermines a key element of SAP's defense that prevailed at the district court level.
   * License Defense Undermined: The Ninth Circuit identified significant ambiguity in the governing agreements (SDCA and TPA) regarding SAP's right to use the 'batched merge' method.40 Crucially, the court found a triable issue as to whether the method constituted a "tool" under the "Partner Materials" clause (Section 10.2), which reserved exclusive rights to Teradata. The court found Teradata's expert testimony supporting this interpretation plausible and rejected SAP's narrower interpretations of "tool".40 The court also questioned whether the method was "Newly Developed Material" (which SAP might own) or pre-existing Teradata IP.40 The introduction of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing as a potential factor further complicates SAP's contractual defense.40
   * Circumstantial Evidence: The narrative presented by Teradata – SAP initiating a joint venture, gaining access to IP, developing the competing HANA product incorporating the alleged trade secret, and then terminating the venture 33 – provides circumstantial evidence supporting the misappropriation claim.
* Strengths for SAP:
   * Contesting Confidentiality: SAP can still argue to the jury that the "Teradata Confidential" marking was insufficient for the specific 'batched merge' details or that the information was not adequately identified as proprietary within the context of the collaboration.40 SAP could also attempt to prove the method was already publicly known or lacked the novelty required for trade secret protection.
   * Asserting License Rights: Despite the Ninth Circuit finding ambiguity, SAP can present its interpretation of the contracts to the jury, arguing that the 'batched merge' method falls under clauses granting SAP usage rights (e.g., as "Input" under Section 9.4 or necessary adaptation code under Section 10.1) and does not qualify as a "tool" reserved to Teradata under Section 10.2.40 SAP can also argue it constituted "Newly Developed Material".40
   * Independent Development Defense: A common defense in trade secret cases is independent development. SAP can present evidence and testimony aiming to demonstrate that its engineers developed HANA's functionalities, including any similar to 'batched merge', independently and without using Teradata's confidential information.43
   * Statute of Limitations: While previously rejected at the pleading stage 37, SAP might explore arguments at trial that Teradata should have been aware of the alleged misappropriation earlier than claimed, potentially impacting the timeliness of the suit under the three-year statute of limitations for DTSA and CUTSA.39
C. Further Considerations (Teradata)
The Ninth Circuit's decision represents a significant validation of Teradata's case, particularly concerning the crucial role of expert economic testimony. The court's detailed defense of Dr. Asker's methodologies 40—including qualitative market definition, aggregate diversion ratio analysis using non-price data, market share analysis, assessment of switching costs, and foreclosure calculations—provides a strong signal that such established economic tools are appropriate for analyzing complex tech markets, even with imperfect data. This not only bolsters Teradata's position significantly on remand by ensuring its core economic evidence is likely admissible, but it also sets a potentially influential precedent within the Ninth Circuit for how courts should evaluate expert economic evidence in tech antitrust cases under the Daubert standard. The decision suggests a potential check on district courts applying overly stringent exclusion standards.85
Similarly, the appellate court's handling of the trade secret claim underscores the importance of contractual ambiguities in surviving summary judgment.40 By finding multiple plausible interpretations of key terms ("clearly identified," "tool," "Partner Materials," "Newly Developed Materials") within the governing NDA and SDCA, the court effectively shifted the resolution of SAP's license defense from the judge to the jury. This highlights the inherent risks for defendants relying solely on contract language to defeat trade secret claims pre-trial, especially when dealing with complex agreements governing technology partnerships and IP rights. The uncertainty of a jury trial now looms larger for SAP on this claim.
Finally, the sheer duration of the Teradata litigation, initiated in 2018 and still active entering 2025 after multiple procedural phases including appeals to two different circuits 32, serves as a stark reminder of the protracted nature of such disputes. Complex antitrust litigation in federal courts often spans multiple years, with typical timelines ranging from three to five years or significantly longer, particularly when appeals are involved.153 While average case durations in N.D. Cal. might appear shorter 162, complex cases involving extensive discovery, expert battles, and multiple motions frequently extend well beyond these averages.154 This lengthy process imposes substantial costs and demands significant management attention, factors that influence strategic decisions regarding settlement versus continued litigation for both SAP and its adversaries.
V. Overarching Challenges for SAP Across Both Litigations
Beyond the specific claims in each case, SAP faces several overarching challenges stemming from the concurrent nature and thematic similarities of the Celonis and Teradata lawsuits. These common threads potentially expose SAP to broader strategic risks.
A. Pattern of Leveraging ERP Dominance
A central narrative emerging from both lawsuits is the allegation that SAP systematically leverages its formidable position in the core ERP market to gain an unfair competitive advantage in adjacent software markets. Both Celonis (in process mining) and Teradata (in EDW) contend that SAP exploits the high switching costs and customer lock-in associated with its ERP systems 2 to force or coerce customers into adopting SAP's offerings in these related fields (Signavio and HANA, respectively).1 This alleged leveraging often involves conditioning necessary ERP upgrades (like the migration to S/4HANA) or ongoing support on the adoption of SAP's adjacent products, while simultaneously restricting the interoperability or data access required for competitors' solutions to function effectively.2 This pattern aligns closely with contemporary antitrust concerns regarding dominant digital platforms using their control over core infrastructure or ecosystems to foreclose competition in complementary or adjacent markets, often referred to as 'ecosystem theories of harm'.88
B. Data Access as a Competitive Lever
Control over customer data residing within SAP's ERP systems emerges as a critical competitive instrument in both disputes. The allegations suggest SAP employs a combination of technical and licensing strategies to manage, restrict, and potentially monetize access to this data in ways that disadvantage third-party software providers like Celonis and Teradata.
* Technical Means: Celonis, in particular, details alleged technical barriers, such as SAP recommending and then deprecating APIs (like OData), banning third-party use of efficient protocols like ODP 73, and using its "Clean Core" policy to effectively prohibit established integration methods (like Celonis's RFC ABAP extractor) while pushing customers towards less viable or more expensive SAP-controlled alternatives like Datasphere.2 These actions can be characterized as a refusal to deal or grant necessary access for interoperability, a key concern in platform antitrust cases.89
* Licensing Means: Both lawsuits touch upon SAP's controversial "Indirect Access" or "Digital Access" licensing policies.2 Allegations include SAP using these policies to impose prohibitive fees on customers using third-party applications that interact with SAP data, applying these fees discriminatorily (e.g., allegedly exempting SAP's own solutions like Signavio, Hybris, or Ariba while charging for competitors 2), and leveraging audits or the threat of audits to coerce customers into accepting unfavorable terms or adopting SAP's preferred solutions.72 This strategy of controlling data flow through licensing has been criticized as anticompetitive "rent-seeking" 5 and resonates with broader debates about data access and platform power in the digital economy.4

	D. Self-Preferencing Acquired Technology
A notable pattern across both lawsuits involves SAP acquiring technology or intellectual property in a market adjacent to its core ERP strength and subsequently facing allegations that it uses its platform dominance to favor this acquired asset over established competitors. In the Celonis case, the acquisition of process mining competitor Signavio in 2021 1 is the explicit catalyst for the alleged exclusionary conduct aimed at Celonis. In the Teradata case, while HANA was developed internally, Teradata alleges its rapid development and success were predicated on the misappropriation of Teradata's IP acquired through the "Bridge Project" joint venture 33, followed by SAP allegedly tying HANA to its core ERP offering to disadvantage Teradata. This pattern echoes concerns frequently raised in modern antitrust discourse about "killer acquisitions"—where dominant firms acquire innovative targets to neutralize a competitive threat—or, more broadly, about leveraging acquisitions to foreclose competition in adjacent markets.135
E. Further Considerations (Overarching)
The parallel nature of the allegations in Celonis and Teradata presents a significant narrative challenge for SAP. Plaintiffs in both cases are likely to argue that SAP employs a consistent "playbook" for entering adjacent software markets: leveraging its ERP dominance, controlling data access through technical and licensing means, and favoring its own (often acquired or allegedly misappropriated) technology to exclude established competitors. This narrative, suggesting a systematic approach rather than isolated business decisions, could make SAP's conduct appear more calculatedly anticompetitive to a judge or jury, potentially increasing scrutiny of its procompetitive justifications. Evidence of similar tactics used against Teradata in the EDW market might be used (subject to rules of evidence) to suggest SAP's intent in its actions against Celonis in the process mining market, and vice versa, thereby amplifying the legal risk beyond the sum of the individual cases.
Furthermore, both lawsuits underscore that in software ecosystems centered around a dominant platform like SAP ERP, the control of data access acts as a critical competitive lever and potential bottleneck. Whether through manipulating APIs 2, imposing restrictive technical standards like Clean Core 2, or utilizing complex and potentially discriminatory licensing policies like Indirect Access/DAAP 2, the ability to dictate how third parties interact with data within the SAP environment is central to the disputes. SAP's evolving policies in this area 72 suggest an ongoing effort to manage this access point. Consequently, these cases could set important precedents regarding the application of antitrust law to data access restrictions within dominant enterprise software ecosystems, potentially influencing the business models and competitive dynamics across the industry.4
VI. Influence of Legal Precedents and Economic Factors
The outcomes of the Celonis and Teradata lawsuits will likely be influenced by existing legal precedents, particularly those involving technology platforms and antitrust law, as well as the strength and admissibility of expert economic testimony presented by both sides.
A. Relevance of Landmark Tech Antitrust Cases (Microsoft, Google, Apple)
The history of antitrust litigation against major technology companies provides a crucial backdrop for understanding the potential legal interpretations and challenges SAP faces.
* United States v. Microsoft Corp.: This landmark case established precedent for holding a dominant technology firm (Microsoft with its Windows OS monopoly) liable under Sherman Act § 2 for using anticompetitive means to maintain its monopoly and disadvantage competitors in adjacent markets (Netscape in the browser market).180 Microsoft's conduct involved technical bundling (integrating Internet Explorer tightly with Windows) and restrictive agreements with OEMs and partners.180 This is highly relevant to allegations that SAP leverages its ERP dominance and restricts API/data access.97 However, the Microsoft case also demonstrated judicial reluctance to impose structural remedies like breakups 180 or to heavily scrutinize product design decisions.111 Furthermore, subsequent Supreme Court decisions, notably Verizon Commc'ns Inc. v. Law Offices of Curtis V. Trinko, LLP, significantly narrowed the scope of the antitrust 'duty to deal,' making it harder for plaintiffs to compel dominant firms to assist rivals.89 The Ninth Circuit in Teradata explicitly considered and distinguished the Microsoft precedent regarding technologically integrated platforms when analyzing the tying claim against HANA.40
* Google Litigation (Search, AdTech, Play Store): The ongoing and recent cases against Google offer relevant parallels concerning allegations of maintaining dominance through exclusionary agreements (e.g., default search deals with Apple and browser makers 189), self-preferencing its own services within its platform 88, and leveraging control over essential platforms (like the Android Play Store) to impose restrictive terms on developers.191 The focus on Google's ecosystem control and alleged anticompetitive exclusion mirrors the claims Celonis and Teradata raise against SAP's ERP ecosystem.111 Recent government successes, such as the DOJ prevailing in the AdTech monopolization case 142 and Epic Games winning its jury trial against the Google Play Store 191, might indicate a greater judicial or juror willingness to find liability against dominant tech platforms for exclusionary conduct, potentially benefiting Celonis and Teradata.
* Apple Litigation (App Store, iPhone): Cases involving Apple provide context for analyzing 'walled garden' ecosystems, control over software distribution (App Store), potential tying arrangements (hardware/software/services), and refusals to interoperate fully with third-party products or services.112 Apple frequently defends its restrictive practices by citing security, privacy, and user experience benefits derived from its integrated ecosystem 112—arguments analogous to potential procompetitive justifications SAP might raise for its Signavio integration or Clean Core policies. The outcome of Epic Games v. Apple was mixed: the court found Apple possessed monopoly power in the relevant market but did not violate federal antitrust laws through its App Store policies, although it did find Apple violated California's Unfair Competition Law (UCL) through its anti-steering provisions.112 This highlights the high bar for proving federal antitrust violations and the potential for state laws to provide alternative avenues for relief. The dismissal of the iCloud monopoly lawsuit further underscores the plaintiff's burden to rigorously define the market and prove monopoly power.197
B. Lessons from Oracle v. SAP and Prior SAP v. Teradata Rulings
* Oracle Corp. v. SAP AG (TomorrowNow): This earlier litigation primarily concerned copyright infringement and trade secret theft by SAP's former subsidiary, TomorrowNow, which provided third-party support for Oracle software.32 It did not involve antitrust claims comparable to those in the Celonis or current Teradata suits. However, the case resulted in a finding of liability against SAP and substantial damages awarded to Oracle (ultimately settling at $356.7 million after a $1.3 billion jury verdict was reduced).32
   * Relevance: The case establishes a history of SAP being involved in high-stakes intellectual property litigation with a major competitor. It demonstrates the potential for significant financial exposure for SAP in IP-related disputes, which is pertinent to Teradata's revived trade secret claim. While not direct precedent for the antitrust issues, the history might subtly color perceptions of SAP's competitive behavior.
* Prior Rulings in Teradata v. SAP (Pre-Appeal): The procedural history within the Teradata case itself offers context. Key pre-appeal rulings included:
   * The district court largely denied SAP's initial motion to dismiss, allowing most claims (including copyright, antitrust, and trade secret) to proceed, although requiring Teradata to specify its trade secrets more clearly.37
   * The same court later granted summary judgment in favor of SAP on the core tying and technical trade secret claims.38 This decision was based heavily on the exclusion of Teradata's expert economic testimony and the court's interpretation of the parties' contracts regarding confidentiality and IP rights.40
   * The court also made rulings related to SAP's patent counterclaims 35 and managed discovery disputes, including those concerning privileged SAP audit materials.55
   * Relevance: These prior rulings illustrate the arguments SAP successfully deployed at the district court level (e.g., challenging the reliability of economic expert methodology, strict interpretation of contractual requirements for confidentiality, asserting broad license rights under the agreements).34 While the Ninth Circuit's reversal significantly weakened these specific arguments in the context of summary judgment, they indicate the likely contours of SAP's defense strategy on remand and potentially at trial.
C. The Pivotal Role of Expert Economic Testimony
* Significance in Teradata: The Ninth Circuit's decision underscores the absolutely critical role of expert economic testimony in substantiating complex antitrust claims like tying.40 The reversal was predicated almost entirely on the finding that the district court erred in excluding Dr. Asker's report.40 The appellate court's validation of his methodologies—spanning market definition (using both qualitative evidence and quantitative tools like aggregate diversion ratios), market power assessment (considering market share, margins, switching costs), and measurement of anticompetitive effects (market foreclosure estimates, pricing analysis, regression models)—demonstrates that such expert analysis is essential for plaintiffs to survive summary judgment and present a viable case to a jury.40
* Implications for Celonis: The Teradata precedent elevates the importance of economic experts for both Celonis and SAP in the newer lawsuit. Celonis will require credible, admissible expert testimony to rigorously define the relevant markets (SAP ERP Data Access, SAP Process Mining), establish SAP's alleged monopoly power within those markets, and quantify the purported anticompetitive harm resulting from SAP's alleged tying, bundling, self-preferencing, and predatory pricing strategies.2 Conversely, SAP will need its own economic experts to vigorously refute Celonis's analysis, propose alternative market definitions, challenge findings of market power, and provide quantitative support for its procompetitive justifications. The litigation will likely feature significant "battles of the experts," including challenges to admissibility under Daubert and intense cross-examination regarding methodologies and conclusions.
* Broader Context: The Ninth Circuit's approach in Teradata suggests a potential willingness within that circuit to accept established economic methodologies applied to the complexities of technology markets, possibly setting a standard that is more receptive to plaintiffs' expert evidence than the initial district court ruling indicated. This could influence how similar evidence is treated in the Celonis case and other tech antitrust litigation within the Ninth Circuit.
D. Further Considerations (Precedents/Economics)
The current legal environment for technology antitrust is dynamic and somewhat uncertain. While foundational cases like Microsoft provide relevant frameworks for analyzing leveraging conduct, subsequent rulings like Trinko have imposed limitations, particularly on refusal-to-deal claims.89 Simultaneously, a new wave of litigation and regulatory scrutiny targeting major digital platforms (Google, Apple, Amazon, Meta) is actively testing the application of antitrust principles to modern platform business models, including issues of self-preferencing, data access, and ecosystem control.111 SAP's defense cannot solely rely on older, potentially more defendant-friendly precedents; the outcomes and reasoning in these ongoing Big Tech cases could significantly shape the legal landscape and influence the trajectory of both the Celonis and Teradata suits.
Against this backdrop, the Teradata appeal decision firmly establishes economic evidence as the linchpin for SAP's antitrust challenges. The Ninth Circuit's detailed engagement with and validation of Dr. Asker's economic analysis 40 confirms that the legal arguments regarding market definition, market power, and competitive effects are inextricably linked to complex economic modeling and proof. This elevates the strategic importance of expert witness work—from initial selection and report preparation through discovery battles and Daubert challenges to trial testimony. Ultimately, the party whose economic narrative proves more persuasive and resilient under judicial scrutiny is likely to prevail on the antitrust claims in both litigations. This points towards potentially protracted and costly expert-driven phases in both cases.
VII. Synthesis and Qualitative Assessment of SAP's Litigation Risk
Synthesizing the analyses of each lawsuit, the common challenges, and the relevant legal context allows for a qualitative assessment of the overall difficulty SAP faces in achieving favorable outcomes across both the Celonis and Teradata litigations.
A. Comparative Assessment: Celonis vs. Teradata
While both cases pose serious threats to SAP, they present different risk profiles and timelines.
* Celonis: This case is characterized by the breadth of its claims, encompassing multiple antitrust theories (monopolization, tying, bundling, predatory pricing) as well as related tort and unfair competition allegations.2 This multi-pronged attack may be complex for SAP to defend against initially. Being in the early stages, the case carries inherent uncertainty; SAP has opportunities for early victories, such as prevailing on its pending Motion to Dismiss.12 However, it also faces the immediate risk of a preliminary injunction 10 that could disrupt its Signavio strategy and data access policies. A particularly potent element is the allegation of broken regulatory promises 2, which, if proven, could significantly damage SAP's credibility. Celonis's success likely depends on surviving dismissal, substantiating its market power and anticompetitive effects claims through expert economic testimony (potentially aided by the Teradata precedent), and convincing the court of SAP's exclusionary intent.
* Teradata: This case, though now more focused on tying and trade secret claims, carries significant momentum following the Ninth Circuit's reversal.40 The appellate court's validation of Teradata's key economic evidence 40 and its findings of triable issues on both the antitrust and trade secret claims 40 substantially weaken SAP's previous defenses. The path to trial appears more direct, placing considerable pressure on SAP to either settle on potentially less favorable terms or face a jury trial on claims that have already demonstrated significant legal resilience. The case's long history since 2018 33 might also influence settlement dynamics.
* Relative Difficulty Assessment: Post-appeal, SAP arguably faces a more immediate and defined threat in the Teradata litigation. The Ninth Circuit has already addressed and rejected key defenses at the summary judgment stage. While Celonis presents a broader array of legal challenges, its earlier procedural posture means SAP has more avenues for seeking dismissal. However, the potential for an early, disruptive preliminary injunction in Celonis and the weight of the "broken promises" narrative add unique risks. Overall, the Teradata case appears closer to a potential trial or resolution, with SAP's position significantly weakened by the appeal outcome.
B. Aggregate Challenge: Defending Both Cases Concurrently
The simultaneous defense of these two major lawsuits presents compounded challenges for SAP:
* Resource Strain: Litigating two complex antitrust and IP cases in the same demanding jurisdiction (N.D. Cal.) 10 requires substantial allocation of legal budgets, internal legal team focus, and senior executive attention.
* Narrative Risk Amplification: The parallel allegations reinforce a potentially damaging narrative that SAP employs a consistent "playbook" of leveraging its ERP dominance and controlling data access to stifle competition in adjacent markets. This perceived pattern could make SAP's actions appear more intentional and anticompetitive than if viewed in isolation. Adverse findings or evidence emerging in one case could potentially influence perceptions or even admissibility in the other.
* Strategic Complexity: SAP must carefully manage its defense strategies across both cases. Arguments made to justify data access policies or integration choices in the Celonis case might need to be reconciled with different factual contexts or claims in the Teradata case. Settlement negotiations are also complicated, as concessions or outcomes in one case could set expectations or perceived precedents for the other.
* Overall Difficulty: Defending either lawsuit alone would be a significant undertaking. Facing both concurrently elevates the overall difficulty substantially. The need to manage distinct procedural tracks, address overlapping thematic risks, and allocate sufficient resources makes achieving favorable outcomes (dismissal, highly advantageous settlement, or trial victory) in both cases simultaneously a formidable challenge.
C. Potential Outcomes Analysis (Qualitative)
* Dismissal: Achieving outright dismissal appears significantly more challenging for SAP in the Teradata case following the Ninth Circuit's reversal, which found triable issues of fact on the core claims.40 While still possible through later motions or at trial, the path is difficult. In Celonis, dismissal is a more immediate possibility via the pending Motion to Dismiss 12, particularly if Celonis fails to adequately plead market definition, monopoly power, or specific elements of its various claims. However, the detailed nature of the complaint 2 and the potential influence of the Teradata precedent on market power analysis suggest dismissal is not guaranteed.
* Favorable Settlement: SAP's leverage to achieve settlement on highly favorable terms is likely diminished in Teradata due to the Ninth Circuit's ruling strengthening Teradata's hand. In Celonis, the outcome of the preliminary injunction motion and the motion to dismiss will heavily influence settlement dynamics. An early win for Celonis on the PI could pressure SAP towards settlement, while a dismissal would strengthen SAP's position. The potential "playbook" narrative across both cases might also incentivize SAP to seek resolution to avoid further adverse findings or discovery.
* Trial Victory: If the cases proceed to trial, SAP faces significant jury risk in both instances. Jurors must grapple with complex economic arguments, technical details of software integration and data access, and potentially damaging narratives about SAP's competitive intent and alleged broken promises or IP theft. While SAP can present strong procompetitive justifications and challenge the plaintiffs' evidence, the validation of Teradata's expert testimony 40 and the breadth of allegations in Celonis 2 indicate that trial victories for SAP are far from assured.
VIII. Conclusion
SAP confronts a challenging legal landscape, defending simultaneously against two substantial lawsuits in the Northern District of California brought by significant competitors, Celonis and Teradata. Both cases allege that SAP has unlawfully leveraged its dominance in the ERP market to stifle competition in adjacent software sectors—process mining in the case of Celonis, and enterprise data warehousing in the case of Teradata.
The Teradata litigation, though older, has gained renewed potency following the Ninth Circuit's reversal of summary judgment in favor of SAP. This decision not only revived Teradata's core claims of illegal tying (S/4HANA and HANA) and trade secret misappropriation ('batched merge' method) but critically validated the admissibility of Teradata's expert economic testimony on market definition and power.40 The appellate court also found triable issues of fact regarding the interpretation of contracts governing confidentiality and IP rights.40 Consequently, SAP faces a difficult path on remand, with key defenses weakened and the prospect of trial looming larger.
The Celonis lawsuit, while procedurally younger, presents a broader and potentially equally potent threat. Celonis alleges a wide array of anticompetitive conduct—including monopolization, tying, bundling, predatory pricing, and false advertising—stemming from SAP's acquisition of Signavio and subsequent changes to data access policies.2 Particularly damaging are the allegations that SAP implemented restrictive technical measures (API changes, Clean Core enforcement) and licensing terms (related to Indirect Access/DAAP) to exclude Celonis, and crucially, that SAP broke specific assurances made to regulators regarding ecosystem openness and third-party data access fees during the Signavio review.2 While SAP has opportunities for early procedural wins, the detailed allegations and the potential resonance of the Teradata precedent regarding economic evidence pose significant hurdles.
Viewed together, the lawsuits paint a picture of potential systemic challenges for SAP. The recurring themes of leveraging ERP dominance and controlling data access as competitive weapons create a narrative risk that transcends either individual case. Defending both complex actions concurrently strains resources and complicates strategic decision-making. While SAP possesses credible defenses, including procompetitive justifications for its product integrations and data policies, the revived strength of the Teradata claims and the potent allegations in the Celonis suit indicate that achieving favorable outcomes (dismissal, highly advantageous settlement, or trial victory) across both fronts will be a substantial undertaking. The evolving landscape of tech antitrust law and the pivotal role of expert economic testimony further contribute to the complexity and uncertainty SAP faces.
